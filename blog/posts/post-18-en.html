Japan Manners &
Etiquette Guide
A Practical Guide for Foreign Visitors [2026]

May 30, 2026Oideya
Japan doesn't expect visitors to get everything right. Small mistakes are generally met with patience, not judgement. But knowing a handful of core customs in advance removes a lot of low-level anxiety — and lets you focus on the trip instead of second-guessing every interaction. This guide covers the etiquette that actually matters, without turning your trip into a minefield of rules.

Shoes: knowing when to take them off

👞

The genkan system

Most Japanese homes, traditional accommodations (ryokan, machiya, kominka), many temples, and some restaurants have a genkan — a slightly recessed entryway at floor level, usually with a shoe rack. This is the universal signal: shoes come off here, and you step up onto the raised floor in socks or slippers.

Slippers are often provided for general indoor use, but there's a further layer: if you enter a tatami-matted room, slippers come off too. Tatami is walked on with socks or bare feet only. Toilet slippers are a separate pair, kept just inside the bathroom door — swap into them before entering, and don't forget to swap back out when you leave (this specific mistake happens to almost every first-time visitor at least once).

DoLook for a genkan and a shoe rack as your cue
Don'tWear toilet slippers outside the bathroom

Bowing and greetings

👋

You don't need to master it

Bowing (ojigi) ranges from a slight nod to a deep formal bow, depending on context and relationship. As a visitor, you're not expected to calibrate this precisely. A small nod of the head when greeting, thanking, or saying goodbye is understood and appreciated — nobody expects tourists to bow like a business executive meeting a client. Handshakes are also increasingly common in tourist-facing contexts, particularly if a Japanese person initiates one.

What matters more than the bow itself is general demeanour: a calm, unhurried manner tends to be read positively across most interactions in Japan.

Chopstick etiquette

🍲

Two rules matter more than the rest

Most chopstick "rules" are minor style points, but two carry real cultural weight because they're associated with funeral rituals:

AvoidSticking chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice (resembles incense at a funeral altar)
AvoidPassing food directly from your chopsticks to someone else's (resembles the passing of cremated bones at a funeral)

Beyond those two: don't point with chopsticks, don't wave them while talking, don't stab food with them like a fork, and rest them on the chopstick holder (hashioki) between bites if one is provided — or laid across the edge of your plate if not.

Onsen (hot spring) etiquette

♨

Wash first, bathe second

The core rule of any Japanese communal bath — onsen, sento (public bathhouse), or a ryokan's bath — is that the shared bathwater is for soaking, not washing. Before entering, sit at the shower stations provided (with soap and shampoo, usually) and wash and rinse thoroughly. Only enter the communal bath once you're clean.

  • No swimsuits. Traditional onsen and sento are entered fully nude, and are gender-separated (look for the character 男 for men's and 女 for women's, or colour-coded curtains — typically blue/dark for men, red/light for women).
  • Tie up long hair so it doesn't touch the water.
  • Small towels stay out of the water — many people fold them and rest them on their head while soaking, rather than dipping them in.
  • Tattoos: many traditional onsen restrict visible tattoos (historically associated with organised crime). Some tourist-oriented facilities are relaxing this, and waterproof cover stickers are sold for smaller tattoos. Check the specific facility in advance if this applies to you.
💡

Common misconception: visitors sometimes assume onsen rules are about modesty. They're really about hygiene and shared-water etiquette — the nudity itself is culturally unremarkable in this specific context, and staring or commenting on other bathers is considered far more impolite than nudity itself.

Trash and recycling

♻

Sorting is serious, but bins are rare

Japan sorts waste into detailed categories — commonly burnable (燃えるゴミ), non-burnable (燃えないゴミ), PET bottles (ペットボトル), cans and glass, and sometimes further subdivisions depending on the municipality. What surprises most first-time visitors is that public trash bins are genuinely scarce on the street, a legacy partly of past security concerns.

The practical response: carry a small bag for your own rubbish through the day, and dispose of it when you find a bin — usually near vending machines, convenience store entrances, or train station platforms. Don't leave litter in public spaces expecting a bin to appear nearby.

Tipping — and why not to

💰

Simply put: don't

Tipping isn't part of Japanese service culture. Excellent, attentive service is the baseline expectation and is already reflected in the price — not something purchased separately. Leaving cash on a restaurant table or trying to tip a taxi driver, hotel staff, or tour guide is usually met with polite confusion, and staff will often chase you down to return the money.

If you want to express particular gratitude for exceptional service, a sincere verbal thank-you ("arigatou gozaimasu") carries more weight than a tip would.

Public behaviour

📡

Quiet is the default

Japan's public spaces — trains, elevators, waiting rooms — run on a generally low-volume, low-disruption standard. This isn't a rigid rule so much as a shared social expectation:

  • Phone calls on trains are considered impolite; step off at the next stop or keep calls very brief and quiet.
  • Eating while walking is uncommon outside of festival food stalls or specific street-food areas (Dotonbori being an exception where it's normalised).
  • Escalators: stand on one side, leave the other clear for people walking (in Osaka, stand right, walk left — the opposite of Tokyo).
  • Queuing is taken seriously and generally orderly — wait your turn, don't push ahead.

None of this means Japan is silent or stiff — conversation, laughter, and normal social energy are completely fine. The distinction is mainly about not imposing noise on strangers in shared, confined spaces.

Gift-giving and business cards

🎁

Mostly relevant for business, not tourism

If you're travelling purely as a tourist, formal gift customs are unlikely to come up. But if your trip involves a business meeting or you're visiting a Japanese friend's home, a couple of points are useful: gifts are typically given and received with both hands, and it's polite not to open a gift immediately in front of the giver unless invited to. Business cards (meishi), similarly, are exchanged with both hands, and it's respectful to study the card briefly rather than pocket it immediately.

The most important rule of all

Genuine effort is noticed and appreciated far more than perfect execution. Getting a bow slightly wrong, hesitating at a genkan, or asking a clarifying question about onsen rules will not offend anyone — it demonstrates that you're paying attention and trying, which is exactly the impression most Japanese hosts and service staff respond well to.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to take off my shoes in Japan?
Yes, in most homes, traditional accommodations (ryokan and machiya), temples, some restaurants, and changing rooms. Look for a genkan (a slightly lowered entryway with a shoe rack) — that's the signal to remove shoes. Slippers are often provided; these should be removed before stepping onto tatami mat flooring, where socks or bare feet are worn instead.
Is tipping expected in Japan?
No. Tipping is not part of Japanese culture and can cause confusion or be politely declined if offered. Excellent service is the standard expectation, already included in the price. This applies to restaurants, taxis, hotels, and tour guides alike.
What are the basic rules for using chopsticks in Japan?
Never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice (this resembles a funeral ritual). Never pass food directly from one set of chopsticks to another (also a funeral association). Don't point with chopsticks or wave them around. Rest them on the chopstick holder (hashioki) when not in use, or across your plate if no holder is provided.
How does onsen (hot spring) etiquette work in Japan?
Wash and rinse thoroughly at the shower stations before entering the communal bath — the bath water is shared and not for washing in. Tie up long hair so it doesn't touch the water. Most traditional onsen require full nudity (no swimsuits) and are gender-separated. Visible tattoos are restricted at many traditional onsen, though this is changing at some newer or tourist-friendly facilities — check in advance.
How does trash and recycling work for visitors in Japan?
Japan sorts waste into multiple categories — typically burnable, non-burnable, PET bottles, and cans/glass — and public trash bins are surprisingly rare, especially on streets. Carry a small bag for your own rubbish until you find a bin, usually near vending machines or convenience stores.
